The overall pattern is a strong one: a country's Rule of Law score (World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators, running roughly −2.5 to +2.5) alone explains about half the cross-country variance in log GDP per capita — consistent with Acemoglu's claim that secure property rights and constraints on expropriation are a "fundamental cause" sitting behind proximate drivers like capital and technology. But the deviations are the more interesting part of the story: resource-rich autocracies (Russia, Belarus, Turkmenistan, Qatar, Guyana) sit well above the trend line — oil, gas, and diamond rents can buy GDP per capita without secure, broad-based property rights. And a cluster of small, remote Pacific island states (Micronesia, Kiribati, Tuvalu, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu) sit well below it — reasonable institutions can't fully offset tiny domestic markets and extreme distance from trade routes. That's the standard critique of a pure institutions story: geography and resource endowments still do real work, they just don't dominate the way they once seemed to.

Source: World Bank Worldwide Governance Indicators (Rule of Law estimate, GOV_WGI_RL.EST) and World Development Indicators (GDP per capita, PPP, current international $), both 2023. Correlation and regression computed from the merged sample of 193 countries with non-missing values on both indicators. This is a cross-sectional correlation, not a causal estimate — AJR's original identification used colonial settler mortality as an instrument for institutions; see "The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development" (2001).
