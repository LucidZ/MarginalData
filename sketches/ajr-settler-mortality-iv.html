Instrumenting institutions: the AJR settler-mortality strategy

Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2001) don't just correlate institutions with income — they use the mortality rate of European colonial settlers as an instrument, to strip out the part of "institutions" that's contaminated by reverse causality. Three linked charts, same 64-country sample, show how.

1First stage — is the instrument relevant?

Where colonial settlers died in droves, Europeans built extractive institutions and left; where they could settle safely, they replicated the property-rights institutions they had at home. If this isn't a strong relationship, the instrument is useless.

Slope −0.61 · R² 0.27 — settler mortality is a strong (if noisy) predictor of institutions.

2Reduced form — any total effect at all?

Settler mortality is a 17th–19th century mortality-table artifact. It shouldn't predict GDP in 1995 through any channel except the institutions it shaped — if there's no relationship here, there's nothing for institutions to transmit.

Slope −0.57 · R² 0.48 — a clear total effect survives four centuries.

3The payoff — OLS vs. instrumented (2SLS)

Gray dots are the raw, uninstrumented data (institutions score as actually measured) — the naive OLS slope is biased by reverse causality (rich countries can afford better courts) and measurement error. Blue dots replace each country's institutions score with its fitted value from panel 1 — only the slice of variation explained by settler mortality survives. That's what "instrumenting" means mechanically, and it's why the slope moves.

Raw institutions score (naive OLS) Instrument-fitted institutions (2SLS)

Naive OLS slope 0.52 → 2SLS slope 0.94 — instrumenting nearly doubles the estimated effect of institutions on income. (This matches AJR's published result almost exactly.)

Caveats worth taking seriously

Disease environment, not just institutions (Sachs, 2003). Settler mortality was driven mostly by malaria and yellow fever. Those same disease burdens plausibly hit 1995 GDP directly — sick agricultural workforces, disease-limited land use — with no institutional channel involved. If so, the exclusion restriction (the instrument affects income only through institutions) fails, and panel 3's slope is partly a geography effect wearing an institutions costume.

The mortality data itself is shaky (Albouy, 2012, AER). AJR's settler-mortality series splices together European soldiers' campaign-mortality records, missionary records, and laborer records — measuring different things in different eras — and roughly half the 64 countries have mortality imputed from "comparable" regions rather than observed directly. Rebuilding the series from primary sources weakens the first-stage relationship in panel 1 substantially.

Two smaller ones: the "institutions" measure may bundle in human capital colonizers also brought (Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer, 2004), and the 64-country sample is small enough that a handful of neo-Europes (United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand — labeled above) sit at the extreme low-mortality, high-income corner and do a lot of the work.

Data: replication files for Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson, "The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development" (American Economic Review, 2001), Table 4 (maketable4.dta), 64-country base sample. Variables: logem4 (log settler mortality), avexpr (average protection against expropriation risk, 1985–95, 0–10 scale), logpgp95 (log GDP per capita, PPP, 1995). Regression lines computed directly from this sample; 2SLS coefficient recovered as OLS of logpgp95 on first-stage-fitted avexpr, which is algebraically identical to two-stage least squares with a single instrument.
